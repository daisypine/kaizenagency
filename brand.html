Why This Exists

Sunwest has been operating since 2001 with a logo, a rough color sense, and no written rules. That works when one person makes everything. It breaks the moment there's a website, a print vendor, a sign shop, and a mail house all producing work at once — which is exactly where the business is now.

Every value in this document was pulled from the approved homepage design, not invented. Nothing here changes the brand. It writes down what the brand already is, so it can be reproduced correctly by anyone.

Logo

Usage Rules

  • Primary lockup — the full horizontal Sunwest Painting logo. Use this everywhere there's room. On the site it sits at 257 × 49 px.
  • Clear space — keep a margin equal to the height of the "S" on all four sides. Nothing intrudes: no text, no photo edge, no border.
  • Minimum size — 120 px wide on screen, 1.25 inches wide in print. Below that the wordmark stops being legible.
  • On photography — use the all-white version over a dark image, or place the logo on a solid panel. Never drop the full-color logo onto a busy photo.
  • Never — stretch it, rotate it, recolor it, add a drop shadow or outline, place it on a color close to its own orange, or rebuild it from a screenshot.
Known issue — fix before the next print run

The version on the old website was a low-resolution file pulled from an old archive. Always source the logo from the master files in Drive or Figma — never from the website, a PDF, or an email. Vendors will use whatever you send them, and a soft logo on a 5,000-piece mailer is expensive to discover late.

The single most recognizable element of the brand. It runs warm yellow into burnt orange at a shallow diagonal, and it appears on every primary button, accent bar, and highlight.

Color Accessibility — Read This Before Designing Anything

Real issue found in the current design

White text on the brand orange does not meet accessibility standards. White on #E07B39 measures a contrast ratio of 2.97:1. The minimum for normal-size text is 4.5:1. On the yellow end of the gradient it's 1.59:1 — far worse.

This matters practically, not just technically: the primary "Request a Quote" button uses white 16px bold text on that gradient. It's the hardest-working element on the site and it's the least readable — especially outdoors on a phone, which is exactly how a homeowner looks at a painter's website.

The fix — use near-black on the brand colors

Black on the brand gradient is dramatically more legible and, on a warm yellow-orange, actually looks better and more premium than washed-out white.

The simple rule

Bright brand colors (400–500) are for fills, bars, and shapes. Dark brand colors (700+) are for text. If a brand color has words sitting on top of it, either the words are near-black or the color is a 700-step or darker. This single rule prevents nearly every legibility problem.

Typography rules

  • Headlines are always uppercase Outfit with negative tracking. That compression is the brand's visual signature — lose it and it stops looking like Sunwest.
  • Never set body copy in Outfit and never set a headline in Poppins. The contrast between the two is the system.
  • Print minimum is 9pt for body copy. Door hangers and postcards get read at arm's length in bad light — go bigger than feels necessary.
  • One headline per piece. A door hanger with three competing headlines communicates nothing.

Change from the current design

The primary button in the Figma file uses white text. This guide specifies Ink 900 instead, for the contrast reason on the previous page. It's a one-line change in the theme, it applies everywhere at once, and it makes the most important button on the site substantially easier to read.

Other components

  • Cards — white fill, 0.58px #515151 border, square corners. Photo on top, Outfit Bold title, Poppins body, full-width action bar at the bottom.
  • Trust badges — 120px circle, icon centered, Poppins SemiBold 20px caption below. Four across: Trusted by Property Managers, Licensed and Insured, Family-Owned, Top Rated in the Bay Area.
  • Accent bars — the gradient as a 6–8px rule. Use to break sections and to top-edge print pieces. The cheapest way to make anything instantly look like Sunwest.

Voice & Messaging

Sunwest doesn't compete on price and shouldn't sound like it does. Competitors currently undercut by $3,000–$5,000 on a typical home. The brand's job is to make that difference feel like a reason to choose Sunwest, not a reason to hesitate.

Positioning in one line

A professional painting company, not a crew with a truck.

Family-owned in San Jose since 2001. Detail-obsessed finish work, real project communication, and a walkthrough at the end. The kind of contractor you hire after a cheap one disappointed you.

The three proof points — use these constantly

  • Craft. They take the time. Trim, doors, custom homes, historic restorations. High-finish work that survives close inspection.
  • Communication. Prep checklists, updates while the job runs, a final walkthrough and sign-off. Most competitors are owner-operators who show up and paint.
  • Credentials. CSLB #807426. Licensed, bonded, insured. EPA RRP certified. In business since 2001.

How it sounds

✓ Do
  • Direct and plain. "We prep more than we paint."
  • Specific. Name the city, the building, the finish.
  • Confident about price. "We're not the cheapest bid. Here's what that buys you."
  • Warm but professional — a family business that runs like a real company.
  • Lead with the customer's problem, not the company's history.
✕ Don't
  • Generic contractor filler — "quality workmanship, satisfaction guaranteed."
  • Discount language, urgency gimmicks, or fake countdowns.
  • Overpromising timelines to win a bid.
  • Exclamation marks stacked in headlines.
  • Leading with "we" in every sentence.

Naming

Written as Sunwest Painting — one word, capital S. Not "Sun West," not "SunWest." The legacy domain and some older material use the two-word form; standardize on one word going forward.

Photography

The brand's biggest visual weakness right now is not design — it's a lack of real photography. Case study pages are built and waiting on images. Stock photos of generic painted walls actively undercut the premium positioning.

What to shoot

  • Before and after pairs, same angle, same time of day. The single most persuasive asset in this trade. Nothing else converts like it.
  • Detail shots. Crisp cut lines, trim, doors, a clean edge against glass. This is the craft argument made visually — it can't be made in words.
  • Crew at work, in branded gear, on a clean and organized site. Drop cloths down, materials staged. Sells professionalism more than any headline.
  • Wide exterior hero shots in late-afternoon light. Straighten verticals.
  • Commercial scale — lifts, large facades, after-hours interiors with the lights on. This is the work homeowners never see and property managers need to.

Treatment

Natural color, no heavy filters, no fake blue skies. Warm afternoon light matches the palette naturally. Keep verticals straight — a leaning building reads as amateur. When placing the logo or text over a photo, darken the image behind it rather than adding an outline to the type.
